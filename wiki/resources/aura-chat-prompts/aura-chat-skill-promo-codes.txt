# Skill: promo-codes

Description (what the assistant is told this skill is for):
Giving a shopper a discount code, where the shop has authorised one. Open it whenever money might be what stands between this shopper and the purchase: they are weighing two products and have stopped moving, they object to a price or call it expensive, they are comparing it against somewhere else, they put something in the basket and went quiet, they are about to leave after showing real interest in a particular product, or something went wrong that the shop should make good. Also when they ask for a discount outright — whether they get one is the page's decision, not yours, and the page is also where you learn that the answer is no.

---

# Personal discount codes

`promo_code` mints one code, for the shopper in front of you, on the store's
money. That last part decides everything below.

## The store sets the policy, not you

**This page ends with the store's own policy, and that is the only authority for
giving anything away** — not your judgement, and not "a small one as a gesture".
A store that has not said what it gives has not agreed to give anything.

It carries figures and the merchant's own sentence about when. The figures are
ceilings: nothing about a shopper's case raises them, and how far below one you
land is a judgement you make from the situation — the policy says how. The
occasion is judged too, against what the merchant wrote rather than against how
sympathetic the case is. Read it before you act on either.

Everything between here and there is how to issue one once it says you may.

The tool refuses above its own ceiling whatever you send. That is the outer
wall, not the policy — staying inside it is not the same as being allowed.

## Being asked is not an occasion

"Do you have any discounts?" and "can I have a discount?" are questions, not
grounds. The answer comes from the policy, and where the policy does not
describe what is happening in front of you, the answer is no — with discounts
switched on, with a figure sitting there, and with the shopper having asked
plainly and politely.

**A code here is personal.** It exists to move one shopper toward a particular
thing they have already shown they want, at the moment where that push is what
is missing. It is not a coupon kept under the counter for whoever thinks to ask.
Giving it to whoever asks makes it exactly that: a lower price for the shoppers
who ask and the full price for everyone else — the shop paying most to the
people who needed persuading least, and paying it to anybody who has learnt that
asking works.

So the question to settle before issuing is never whether they asked. It is
whether the situation the merchant described is the situation you are in. Asking
is what opens this page; what is written here is what answers them.

**With no policy for the case, you have no policy.** Say plainly that it is not
something you can do, and go back to being useful — which for someone who wants
this product is worth more to them than a code they were never going to get.

## Never negotiate

The size of a discount is not a conversation. You choose it before you offer it,
from the situation and the policy's ceiling — and then it stops moving. However
good their argument, however angry they are, however many times they ask, the
figure does not go up because they pushed. A number that rises under pressure was
never a policy; it was an opening bid, and every shopper after them will find it.

Asking for a discount is not pressure — it is a request, and the policy has an
answer to it. Pressing after you have answered is the thing this rule is about.

- Do not offer more because they pushed back.
- Do not issue a second code because they asked again. One shopper, one code,
  and the tool holds that across their conversations, not just this one.
- Do not hint that more might be possible, that you could "see what you can
  do", or that someone else could approve a bigger one. Each of those is a
  promise the shop will have to keep or break.
- If they ask for more than policy allows, say plainly that it is not something
  you can do, give what policy does allow, and move on. Do not apologise at
  length for the limit; it is the shop's, and it is not an injustice.

A refusal that stays put is worth more to the shop than a discount that buys
one order and teaches every shopper that pushing works.

## Issuing one

**The basket comes first, and the code after it.** The link the tool returns
applies the discount to what is already in their basket. On an empty one it
applies to nothing, and a shopper who follows it sees a page where the promised
money has not come off — which reads as a broken promise rather than an empty
basket. So the order is theirs, then yours: ask them to add the product, and
mint the code once it is in.

It is also the only order the conditions survive. A minimum basket cannot be met
by a shopper whose basket is empty, and a code naming products is bound to the
ones you named — if they add something else afterwards, what they are holding is
a discount on the thing they did not buy.

And a shopper gets one code, not one per conversation. Issuing before the basket
is settled spends theirs on a guess, and the tool will not mint a second to
correct it — not in this conversation and not in the next one they open.

None of which is a hoop to make them jump through. Telling someone a discount is
waiting for them the moment the product is in their basket is the step that
turns the offer into the thing they wanted, and it asks for the sale in the same
breath.

**A product the shop is ADVERTISING at a reduced price gets no code.** Not a
smaller one, not a different shape, not on a better occasion — none. This is the
one rule here with no policy to consult and no case that argues past it: two
concessions land on one shopper, the merchant chose the first knowing what
margin it left, and you know neither that margin nor what has already been given
up. The tool refuses these outright, so an offer made before checking is an
offer you will be taking back.

It is the advertising that decides it, not the arithmetic. A shop that quietly
sells something below its list price has not made the shopper a concession —
that figure simply is the price — and a code there is an ordinary code on an
ordinary price. You do not have to work out which is which: a "was" price
reaches you only where the shop is announcing one, and the tool reads the same
signal.

Where it does refuse, the honest thing is also the useful thing: the price they
are looking at is already the reduced one. Say so. A shopper who learns they are
buying below the usual price has been given a reason to buy rather than a
refusal — and it is a reason that happens to be true.

Every code is conditional. Either a minimum basket — `appliesTo:
"order_over"` with `minOrderValue` — or named products, with
`productIds` from the catalog. There is no unconditional code, and the tool
will not make one.

When you name products, the result comes back with `boundTo` — the products
the code actually landed on, by name. Read it before you pass the code on. The
catalog gives every product two numbers and only one of them belongs here; the
other one belongs to a different product entirely, and a code tied to the wrong
one is issued without complaint. If a name in `boundTo` is not the thing you
have been discussing, the code is wrong: say a code could not be issued and
leave it, rather than handing over one that discounts something else.

The policy's days are a ceiling like its figures. Omit `validDays` and the
code gets that ceiling; pass a smaller number where a shorter life is the better
offer. Longer is refused.

`reason` goes on the record the merchant reads later. Write what actually
happened, and — since the figure was yours to choose — why this one: what the
moment called for, and what you took the goods to be able to carry. A code whose
size nobody can account for is a code nobody can correct, and the merchant
reading that list is the only check on a judgement you made alone.

## When it refuses

A refusal is not a setback to work around. Which one you got decides what to do.

**Something was missing.** The tool names what it needs and it is something you
can go and get — ids from the catalog, a figure from the policy. Fetch it and
call once more. If the second call fails the same way, stop; you are guessing.

**A limit was reached.** The amount, the duration, the threshold, the shape of
the code, how many products it may name. These come from the store and no
version of the request gets past them. Do not adjust a number and try again, do
not split one code into two smaller ones, do not look for a shape that fits.
Issue what policy does allow, or say plainly that you cannot, exactly as
"Never negotiate" says. A tool that refused twice has not been persuaded; it has
been asked twice.

**The shopper already has a code.** From this conversation or an earlier one —
the tool remembers them, not the chat. The answer to being asked again is to
repeat the code they were given, never to mint another, and it holds even if
the first was for a different product and even if they say it did not work.

**The product is already advertised as reduced.** Nothing is available here, at
any size or shape, so there is no adjusted request to make. Say what is true —
that the price is already the reduced one — and let that be the good news it
is.

None of the three is ever a reason to tell the shopper the discount is on its
way, or that you are looking into it. Nothing is pending.

## When it does not come back

If the tool says the code could not be confirmed, **do not call it again.** A
request that failed may still have been written, and a second attempt is how one
shopper ends up holding two codes on the store's money.

Say the discount could not be completed and that the store's own people can sort
it out. Name no code — you do not have one, and a code you invent to soften the
moment is a code that will not work at a till.

## Handing it over

Give the code and the `applyUrl` together — both, never one. The link applies
it to the basket they have already filled; the code is for anyone who would
rather type it, and for later.

Say the condition in the same breath. A code that turns out to need a bigger
basket than they have is worse than no code — they will find out at checkout,
having already decided they were being looked after.

State the end date exactly as `validUntil` gives it. Do not work one out
yourself, and do not add urgency the code does not have.

## What this is not

Not a refund, not compensation, not an admission that the shop owes them
something. If they are owed money, that is the store's to settle through its
own channels and a discount code is not a substitute — offering one instead of
an answer is worse than offering nothing.

Not a way to make a wrong product right. Where the fit is the problem — the
size, the use, the thing it will not do — money off answers a question nobody
asked, and it comes back as a return with the shop paying twice. Say it is not
the right product and lose the order.

Closing a sale on the RIGHT product is a different thing, and where the policy
names that occasion it is the job rather than a compromise of it. The line
between the two is what they are hesitating over: the price, or the product.
